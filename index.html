00. The Accounting Cycle — where all of this starts

Before P&L or ratios mean anything, every transaction a business makes travels through the same four stops. This is the backbone of every UG Financial Accounting paper (BCU Sem 1 included), and it's worth having cold.

Journal→Ledger→Trial Balance→Final Accounts

The Accounting Equation

Assets = Liabilities + Capital

Every single transaction changes at least two things on this equation, and it must always stay balanced — that's the entire logic of double-entry bookkeeping. Buy machinery with cash: one asset (cash) falls, another asset (machinery) rises. Take a loan: cash (asset) rises, and the loan (liability) rises with it.

The Three Types of Accounts and their Golden Rules

Account typeCoversGolden rule
PersonalPeople, firms, companies (e.g. "Ramesh's A/c")Debit the receiver, Credit the giver
RealAssets — cash, machinery, buildingsDebit what comes in, Credit what goes out
NominalExpenses, incomes, gains, lossesDebit all expenses & losses, Credit all incomes & gains

Sunrise Traders pays ₹50,000 rent in cash: Rent A/c (Nominal, an expense) is debited; Cash A/c (Real, cash going out) is credited.

Every journal entry gets posted to individual ledger accounts (one account per head — Cash, Rent, Sales, etc.). Once all ledgers are totalled, their balances are listed side by side in a Trial Balance — a check that total debits equal total credits before the final statements (Trading A/c, P&L A/c, Balance Sheet) are drawn up.

Depreciation — spreading the cost of an asset

Machinery, vehicles, and equipment lose value every year through wear and use. Depreciation is the accounting method for spreading that cost across the asset's useful life instead of expensing the full amount the day it's bought.

Straight Line Method (SLM)

(Cost of Asset − Scrap Value) ÷ Useful Life (years)

A machine bought for ₹10 lakh, with a ₹1 lakh scrap value and a 9-year life, depreciates by a flat ₹1 lakh every year.

Written Down Value Method (WDV)

Depreciation Rate % × Book Value at the start of the year

At 10% WDV on a ₹10 lakh machine: Year 1 depreciation is ₹1 lakh (book value now ₹9 lakh); Year 2 depreciation is 10% of ₹9 lakh = ₹90,000. The depreciation amount shrinks every year because it's charged on the reducing balance, not the original cost.

1. The Profit & Loss Statement (P&L)

The P&L — also called the Income Statement — answers one question: did the company make money this year? It's not about what the company owns; it's about what came in and what went out.

Revenue−COGS=Gross Profit
Gross Profit−Operating Expenses=EBITDA
EBITDA−Depreciation & Amortization=EBIT (Operating Profit)
EBIT−Interest−Tax=Net Profit

Line by line

Revenue (Sales) — total money earned from selling goods or services, before anything is subtracted.

COGS (Cost of Goods Sold) — the direct cost of producing what was sold: raw material, direct labour. Doesn't include rent or marketing.

Gross Profit — Revenue minus COGS. Tells you how efficiently the core product is made or delivered.

Operating Expenses (OpEx) — rent, salaries, marketing, admin — the cost of running the business day to day.

EBITDA — Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation & Amortization. It is an operating-performance measure before financing, tax and non-cash D&A effects. It is not the same thing as cash flow because working capital, capital expenditure and other cash items are still missing.

Depreciation & Amortization — spreading the cost of a long-term asset (machinery, software) over its useful life instead of expensing it all at once.

EBIT — Earnings Before Interest and Tax. Same as "Operating Profit."

Net Profit (PAT) — what's actually left for shareholders after interest and tax. The famous "bottom line."

Sunrise Traders, FY26: Revenue ₹10 crore − COGS ₹6 crore = Gross Profit ₹4 crore. Minus OpEx ₹1.5 crore = EBITDA ₹2.5 crore. Minus depreciation ₹30 lakh = EBIT ₹2.2 crore. Minus interest ₹20 lakh and tax ₹50 lakh = Net Profit ₹1.5 crore.

2. The Balance Sheet

If the P&L is a video of the year, the Balance Sheet is a photograph taken on one specific date. It shows what the company owns (Assets), what it owes (Liabilities), and what's left for the owners (Equity).

Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity

This always balances — it's not a coincidence, it's the accounting identity. Every asset is funded either by someone else's money (liability) or the owner's own money (equity).

The three buckets
BucketWhat it meansExamples
AssetsWhat the company owns or is owedCash, inventory, receivables, property, machinery
LiabilitiesWhat the company owes to othersLoans, supplier payables, bonds issued
EquityThe owners' residual stakeShare capital + retained (accumulated) profits

Current vs. Non-current: Assets and liabilities are split by time horizon. "Current" means it'll convert to cash or come due within 12 months (cash, inventory, short-term debt). "Non-current" is longer-term (property, long-term loans). This split is the entire basis for liquidity ratios later in this guide.

3. The Cash Flow Statement

Profit on paper isn't the same as cash in the bank — a company can show a healthy Net Profit and still run out of cash if customers haven't paid yet. This statement tracks actual cash movement, in three buckets:

SectionTracksExample
OperatingCash from core business activityCash collected from customers, paid to suppliers/staff
InvestingCash used to buy or sell long-term assetsBuying machinery, selling an old office building
FinancingCash from lenders or shareholdersTaking a loan, repaying debt, paying dividends

Why this matters for you as a future advisor: a client's portfolio company can look profitable on the P&L and still be a red flag if operating cash flow is consistently negative — that's often the first sign of trouble analysts look for.

The 30-second mental model

When you open a set of financials, do not start by memorising ratios. Ask five questions in this order:

01Did it make money?P&L / Income Statement
02What does it own and owe?Balance Sheet
03Did cash actually move?Cash Flow Statement
04How good is the performance?Margins, returns, liquidity, efficiency
05How is the market pricing it?P/E, P/B, EV/EBITDA + risk

Cost Accounting Basics

Financial Accounting tells you if the whole business made money. Cost Accounting goes one level deeper — what does it actually cost to make one unit of the product, and where can that cost be trimmed?

Elements of Cost — building up to Total Cost

Direct Material + Direct Labour + Direct Expenses=Prime Cost
Prime Cost+Factory Overheads=Factory/Works Cost
Works Cost+Office & Admin Overheads=Cost of Production
Cost of Production+Selling & Distribution Overheads=Total Cost

A Cost Sheet simply lays these out top to bottom for a given period or a given quantity, ending in Cost per Unit and, if a selling price is added, Profit per Unit.

Break-Even Analysis

The point at which a business neither makes a profit nor a loss — total revenue exactly equals total cost.

Break-Even Point (units) = Fixed Costs ÷ (Selling Price per Unit − Variable Cost per Unit)

Sunrise Traders has ₹20 lakh fixed costs, sells at ₹500/unit, and variable cost is ₹300/unit. Contribution per unit = ₹200. BEP = ₹20,00,000 ÷ ₹200 = 10,000 units — below this, the business runs at a loss.

Margin of Safety = Actual Sales − Break-Even Sales

Tells you how far actual sales can fall before the business tips into loss — a cushion figure investors and lenders both watch.

Time Value of Money & Capital Budgeting

A rupee today is worth more than a rupee tomorrow — this idea underpins almost all of finance

Future Value & Present Value

FV = PV × (1 + r)ⁿ   |   PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)ⁿ

₹1 lakh invested today at 10% for 3 years grows to FV = 1,00,000 × (1.10)³ = ₹1,33,100. Flip it: ₹1,33,100 receivable in 3 years is worth only ₹1 lakh to you today, at a 10% discount rate.

Net Present Value (NPV)

NPV = (Sum of Discounted Future Cash Inflows) − Initial Investment

A positive NPV means the discounted cash inflows exceed the initial investment at the chosen discount rate. A negative NPV means they fall short. In a real decision, NPV should be read alongside assumptions, risk, capital constraints and alternative uses of funds.

Internal Rate of Return (IRR)

The discount rate at which NPV becomes exactly zero — effectively, "what return does this project actually generate?" Compare IRR to your required rate of return (cost of capital) to decide.

Payback Period

Initial Investment ÷ Annual Cash Inflow

Simplest of the lot — how many years to recover the original investment. It ignores cash flows after payback and, in the basic form shown here, ignores time value of money. That makes it a quick recovery measure rather than a complete investment valuation method.

Leverage & Capital Structure

Leverage measures how much fixed costs (operating) or fixed-interest debt (financial) magnify the swings in profit when sales change.

Operating Leverage

Contribution ÷ EBIT

High operating leverage (high fixed costs relative to variable costs) means profit swings widely with small changes in sales volume — common in capital-intensive manufacturing.

Financial Leverage

EBIT ÷ EBT (Earnings Before Tax)

Measures how much debt in the capital structure magnifies the impact of EBIT changes on EPS. More debt = higher financial leverage = higher risk to equity shareholders, but potentially higher ROE if the business does well (this is the "double-edged sword" of debt).

Combined Leverage

Operating Leverage × Financial Leverage

Captures the combined effect of operating and financial leverage on the sensitivity of earnings to sales. Higher combined leverage means a larger earnings response to a given change in sales, all else equal.

Working Capital Management

Net Working Capital = Current Assets − Current Liabilities

This is the money tied up in day-to-day operations — inventory sitting on shelves, money owed by customers (receivables), minus what's owed to suppliers (payables). Too little working capital risks not being able to pay near-term bills (this is exactly what the Current and Quick Ratios below measure); too much means cash is sitting idle instead of earning a return.

Gross Working Capital is simply the total of all current assets, with no liabilities netted off — used less often in analysis than Net Working Capital, but appears in some exam definitions.

4. Profitability Ratios

How well the business turns sales into profit

Gross Profit Margin

Gross Profit ÷ Revenue × 100

Sunrise Traders: ₹4 cr ÷ ₹10 cr = 40%. For every ₹100 in sales, ₹40 is left after direct production costs.

Net Profit Margin

Net Profit ÷ Revenue × 100

₹1.5 cr ÷ ₹10 cr = 15%. This is the "real" margin — after every expense, interest, and tax.

Return on Equity (ROE)

Net Profit ÷ Shareholders' Equity × 100

If Sunrise has ₹7.5 cr equity: ₹1.5 cr ÷ ₹7.5 cr = 20%. This is the number equity investors care about most — how hard is my money working?

Return on Assets (ROA)

Net Profit ÷ Total Assets × 100

Tells you how efficiently the company uses its asset base to generate profit. Compare ROA across companies with similar business models and accounting structures; capital intensity and industry economics matter.

Return on Capital Employed (ROCE)

EBIT ÷ (Total Assets − Current Liabilities) × 100

Measures operating return on the capital tied up in the business. Because definitions vary, use a consistent capital-employed formula when comparing companies, and compare firms with similar business models.

5. Liquidity Ratios

Can the business pay its near-term bills?

Current Ratio

Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities

A ratio of 2.0 means the company has ₹2 in short-term assets for every ₹1 of short-term debt. Below 1 is a warning sign — it may not be able to pay bills due within a year.

Quick Ratio (Acid-Test Ratio)

(Current Assets − Inventory) ÷ Current Liabilities

Stricter than the Current Ratio — it strips out inventory, since inventory isn't always quickly convertible to cash. This is the "can you pay right now, without selling stock" test.

6. Leverage / Solvency Ratios

How much of the business is funded by debt, and can it be serviced?

Debt-to-Equity (D/E)

Total Debt ÷ Shareholders' Equity

A D/E of 0.5 means the company has ₹0.50 of debt for every ₹1 of owner's equity. Compare D/E with similar companies and industry norms; the ratio is not directly comparable across every sector, and financial institutions often require sector-specific leverage measures.

Interest Coverage Ratio

EBIT ÷ Interest Expense

Shows how many times operating profit covers the interest bill. Lower coverage means less cushion; there is no universal cut-off because industry economics, debt terms and business cycles differ.

7. Efficiency Ratios

How well the business uses its resources

Inventory Turnover

COGS ÷ Average Inventory

Higher usually means inventory is sold and replaced quickly — good for cash flow. Very high can also mean stockouts, so context (industry norms) matters.

Asset Turnover

Revenue ÷ Total Assets

How much revenue is generated per rupee of assets owned. Retail and FMCG companies tend to run high asset turnover; utilities and infra run low.

8. Valuation Ratios (for equity/stock analysis)

Is the stock cheap or expensive relative to its fundamentals?

Price-to-Earnings (P/E)

Market Price per Share ÷ Earnings per Share (EPS)

A stock trading at ₹200 with EPS of ₹10 has a P/E of 20 — investors are paying ₹20 for every ₹1 of annual earnings. Always compare P/E to industry peers, not in isolation.

Price-to-Book (P/B)

Market Price per Share ÷ Book Value per Share

Book value = Equity ÷ number of shares. Useful for asset-heavy businesses like banks and NBFCs, where the balance sheet itself is the product.

Dividend Yield

Dividend per Share ÷ Market Price per Share × 100

Shows annual dividend income relative to the current share price. It does not include capital gains or losses, and the yield changes when the market price changes.

EV/EBITDA (Enterprise Value to EBITDA)

(Market Cap + Debt − Cash) ÷ EBITDA

A cleaner comparison across companies with different debt levels than P/E — commonly used in PE/M&A and cross-company comparisons.

9. Ratios specific to wealth & portfolio advisory

These sit on top of everything above — they measure portfolios, not companies

Since your target role is HNI advisory, these matter as much as the company-level ratios — and they're the ones your WealthAxis project and NISM syllabus will lean on hardest.

Sharpe Ratio

(Portfolio Return − Risk-Free Rate) ÷ Standard Deviation of Portfolio Return

Measures return earned per unit of risk taken. Two portfolios can have the same return, but the one with a higher Sharpe Ratio got there with a smoother ride — less volatility along the way. This is the number you'll use constantly when comparing fund options for a client.

Alpha

Actual Portfolio Return − Expected Return (based on its Beta and the market's return)

Positive alpha means realised return exceeded the model or benchmark-adjusted return implied by the chosen framework. It is not proof of skill by itself; benchmark choice, time period, model assumptions and fees matter.

Beta

Covariance(Portfolio, Market) ÷ Variance(Market)

Measures sensitivity to benchmark movements. A Beta of 1.2 means the portfolio has historically shown about 1.2× the benchmark's sensitivity in the chosen measurement period. Higher Beta means greater systematic market sensitivity, not a guaranteed higher return.

Standard Deviation

The classic measure of volatility — how much returns swing around their average. Higher standard deviation = a bumpier ride, which matters enormously when profiling a conservative HNI client versus an aggressive one.
